# M2 experiment: BM25 evidence retrieval (general-purpose seed corpus)

**Date:** 2026-11-03 · **Snapshot:** 97 documents, 1,090 passages (1000 / 200 / 0.5) · **Gold set:** `data/eval/claims.jsonl`, 50 claims

Reproduce with (no download needed; corpus texts are committed):

```bash
uv run python -m verity ingest --overwrite   # build data/snapshots/current
uv run python -m verity evaluate             # headline metrics
uv run python scripts/m2_experiments.py      # everything below (~7 s)
```

> **This report replaces an earlier M2 report** that was run on a six-document, single-topic corpus (commit `a8489c8`). Those numbers are not comparable with the ones below: the corpus, the gold set and the analyzer default have all changed. The methodology lessons carried over; see "What changed from the first M2 run" at the end and `docs/PROGRESS.md`.

**How to read these numbers:**
- The gold set is small: 41 answerable claims.
- A single claim can move MRR by up to 0.024 (1/41). Differences smaller than that are noise unless the bootstrap interval says otherwise.
- Nothing was tuned on this set. The parameter sweep and the ablations are reported for understanding only.

## 1. Setup

| | |
|---|---|
| Corpus | 17 English Wikipedia + 5 Simple English Wikipedia articles (pinned revisions, CC BY-SA 4.0), 75 SciFact abstracts (ODC-By 1.0). See `data/corpus/README.md` |
| Retriever | BM25 from scratch with an inverted index (`src/verity/retrieval/bm25.py`); k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75, Lucene idf |
| Analyzer | NFKC → casefold + accent fold → letter/digit tokens → stopwords → S-stemmer. PDF-artifact repair is available but **off by default** (section 5, ADR-011) |
| Claims | 50 claims in 13 topics; see the composition table below |
| Relevance | A passage is relevant if it fully contains a gold evidence quote (ADR-012) |
| Metrics | Recall@k: share of a claim's evidence quotes found in the top k, averaged over claims. MRR: 1/rank of the first relevant passage. Both are computed over the 41 answerable claims |
| Speed | Index build 100 ms; 0.2 ms per query; vocabulary of 12,949 terms |

### Gold-set composition

| origin | supported | contradicted | conflicting | insufficient | total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| FEVER dev (re-verified against the pinned revisions) | 9 | 10 | – | 4 | 23 |
| SciFact dev (seeded, label-stratified sample) | 6 | 5 | – | 5 | 16 |
| WikiContradict (contradiction inside one article) | – | – | 8 | – | 8 |
| Written for Verity (cross-source) | 1 | – | 2 | – | 3 |
| **total** | 16 | 15 | 10 | 9 | 50 |

- **88 evidence quotes** in total, about 2.1 per answerable claim. 16 of them sit in two passages because of chunk overlap.
- **Phrasing is measured, not annotated** (ADR-012). A claim is *lexical* if at least half of its index terms occur in its closest evidence quote. By that rule, 23 claims are lexical and 18 are paraphrases.
- **Conflicts between sources.** 7 claims have evidence in more than one document. Two of them are real conflicts *between* sources:
  - Yandex's launch year: English vs Simple English Wikipedia.
  - The end date of the Cretaceous: 66 vs 65 million years ago.
- **R@1 ceiling: 0.648.** 29 claims have evidence spread over several passages, so even a perfect ranking cannot put all of it at rank 1. MRR has no such ceiling.

**Every answerable claim has at least one relevant passage in the top 10.** The misses are partial: R@10 = 0.896 because some claims have several quotes and not all of them rank.

**Both sides of a conflict.** Of the 10 conflicting claims, both the supporting and the contradicting evidence were in the top 5 for 6 claims, in the top 10 for 7, and in the top 20 for all 10. That is better than it sounds, because 8 of the 10 are contradictions *inside one article*, so both sides share the article's vocabulary. The two cross-source conflicts are harder:

- **verity-01 (Yandex launch).** The two English quotes rank 1 and 3; the contradicting Simple English quote ranks 9.
- **verity-02 (Cretaceous end date).** The contradicting Simple English quote ranks 1 and the English one ranks 2.

### Before and after pooling

The first evaluation of this gold set scored **R@1 0.398, R@5 0.821, R@10 0.896, MRR 0.747**. I then reviewed the top-ranked non-gold passages for every claim whose rank-1 hit was not gold, and the top hits for every insufficient claim. This is "pooling", the standard TREC method, and it is the same procedure as in the first M2 run. Only **direct** evidence was added. Each addition is marked in the claim's `note` ("Gold extended by pooling."):

| claim | added evidence |
|---|---|
| sci-10 "CX3CR1 on the Th2 cells suppresses airway inflammation." | A second contradicting sentence from the same abstract ("CX3CR1 signaling promoted T(H)2 survival … restored asthma"). SciFact did not annotate this sentence as a rationale |
| fever-01 "Elizabeth of York's father was Edward IV." | "as the eldest child of King Edward IV and his wife, Elizabeth Woodville" |
| fever-11 "There are no musical or creative works … by Phillip Glass." | Two more contradicting statements. Almost any passage of the article contradicts this claim, so the gold set lists representative quotes, not all of them |
| fever-21 "A near-Earth object is a small Solar System body." | The formal IAU definition, and "like all other small Solar System bodies" |
| fever-23 "A near-Earth object is abbreviated as NGO." | The definition passage, which gives the abbreviation NEO |

Considered and **not** added, because the link to the claim needs an inference step:

- **fever-02.** The Simple English article says Richard III was the uncle of Elizabeth's *brothers*.
- **fever-07.** One group of marine reptiles, the ichthyosaurs, went extinct within the Cretaceous.
- **fever-20.** "An athlete's heart rate can be lower than 60 bpm" does not state an effect of exercise.
- **sci-14.** The retrieved abstract describes B cells moving to *follicular* niches, while the claim is about *paracortical* areas and oxysterols.

Pooling also checked every insufficient claim. For each one, a corpus-wide search for its key terms found no relevant text: clpC, myeloid, oxysterol, type 1 diabetes, secondary mediators, Belgium (in the Yandex articles), heart attacks (Elizabeth of York), and a height comparison with the Himalayas.

After pooling: **MRR 0.803** (+0.056), R@1 0.423, R@5 0.837, R@10 0.896.

**Caveat:** pooling from one system favours that system. Passages BM25 never ranked highly were never reviewed. The gold set must be re-pooled with every new retriever (M6).

## 3. Reference implementation: rank_bm25

The `okapi` idf variant reproduces `rank_bm25.BM25Okapi` (k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75, ε = 0.25) exactly:

| analyzer | max \|score difference\| over 50 queries × 1,090 passages | identical top 10 |
|---|---|---|
| raw | 3.4 × 10⁻¹³ | 50 / 50 |
| default | 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁴ | 50 / 50 |

| configuration | R@1 | R@5 | R@10 | MRR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ours, Lucene idf, k1 = 1.2 (default) | 0.423 | 0.837 | 0.896 | 0.803 |
| ours, Okapi idf, k1 = 1.5 (rank_bm25 settings) | 0.423 | 0.837 | 0.896 | 0.801 |

The difference is −0.002 MRR, 95% interval [−0.006, 0.000], so the two settings are indistinguishable here. No term occurs in more than half the passages, so Okapi's negative-idf floor never applies.

## 4. Analyzer ablations

| configuration | R@1 | R@5 | R@10 | MRR | ΔMRR vs default [95% CI] |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| raw (casefold + tokenize) | 0.484 | 0.884 | 0.913 | 0.871 | **+0.067 [+0.017, +0.131]** |
| + stopwords | 0.447 | 0.852 | 0.888 | 0.821 | +0.017 [−0.007, +0.055] |
| **default** (+ S-stemming) | 0.423 | 0.837 | 0.896 | 0.803 | — |
| default + PDF repair | 0.423 | 0.837 | 0.896 | 0.803 | 0.000 |
| default without stopwords | 0.435 | 0.862 | 0.909 | 0.827 | +0.024 [−0.001, +0.068] |
| default without stemming | 0.447 | 0.852 | 0.888 | 0.821 | +0.017 [−0.007, +0.055] |

The intervals come from a paired bootstrap over claims (10,000 resamples).

**On this corpus, the raw analyzer beats the default, and the interval excludes zero.** The difference comes from 7 of 41 claims, each moving by 1–4 ranks. No claim gets worse with the raw analyzer. Two mechanisms explain most of it:

- **FEVER claims copy the definition pattern.** FEVER annotators wrote claims from sentences in an article's introduction. "Yandex is a newspaper" matches the definition "Yandex … is a Russian technology company …" only through `is` and `a`, which the default analyzer removes as stopwords. Rank: 1 raw, 5 default.
- **Stemming can blur a specific form.** "Phillip Glass has written eleven concertos" should find "12 concertos". With stemming, `concerto` also matches every passage about a single concerto. Rank: 1 raw, 3 default.

**The default was not changed.** The first M2 run, on a different corpus, found no significant difference in either direction. Switching analyzers on the evidence of 7 claims from one evaluation set would be tuning on the test set. The question is recorded in PROGRESS.md: decide it on a held-out split, or after dense retrieval (M6) changes what lexical retrieval is for.

## 5. Extraction artifacts and the PDF repair rules

The new corpus contains no PDFs: Wikipedia text comes from MediaWiki's parsed HTML, and SciFact text comes from JSON. The PDF artifact patterns barely occur:

| pattern | matches | documents |
|---|---:|---:|
| kerning split ("T able") | 39 | 16 |
| line-break hyphen ("re-\nduced") | 0 | 0 |
| spacing diacritic ("Dvoˇ rák") | 0 | 0 |
| ligature (ﬁ ﬂ ﬀ ﬃ ﬄ) | 0 | 0 |
| page break (\f) | 0 | 0 |

**All 39 kerning-rule matches are false.** They are real one-letter words, not split words:
- "T cells" ×15 and "T cell" ×12 → `tcell`
- "T helper" → `thelper`
- "T extinction" (Cretaceous–Paleogene) → `textinction`
- "Saturn V rocket", "T wave", "T zone", "tarsomere V on"
- Roman numerals, such as "Edward V was" → `vwa`

The rule was designed on PDF text, where a detached capital ("T able") really is a split word. It cannot tell the two cases apart. **PDF repair is therefore off by default** (ADR-011 updated). It stays available as `Analyzer(repair_pdf_artifacts=True)` for PDF-derived text, and turning it on changes no claim's rank here. Only 3 passages (0.3%) are at least 25% numbers; they make up 0.4% of top-10 results.

## 7. Long-document and source-share effects

| collection | share of all top-10 results | share of passages | mean passage length |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| English Wikipedia | 77.4% | 82.6% | 81.4 terms |
| Simple English Wikipedia | 4.0% | 1.2% | 73.9 terms |
| SciFact | 18.6% | 16.2% | 81.3 terms |

- **No single document dominates.** The largest documents have 91–115 passages each. The biggest top-10 shares are Elizabeth of York at 8.4% and Acne at 7.8%. Chunking puts each passage in competition on its own, so a document's length does not inflate its rank.
- **Simple English articles are over-represented** in the top 10, at three times their share of passages. Their short, plain sentences resemble how claims are phrased. The general-audience article in the first M2 run showed the same effect.
- **Overlap redundancy.** In 61 cases across 50 queries, two adjacent top-10 results were overlapping passages of the same document. Overlap still pays off: 16 evidence quotes lie in an overlap region and so are contained in two passages, and a quote that crosses a passage boundary would otherwise sit in no single passage at all. But overlap fills top-k slots with repeated text. A later stage should merge overlapping hits.

## 8. Source confusion

Share of top-5 hits that come from a document holding evidence for the claim, and from the other collection (encyclopedia vs abstracts):

| claims from | evidence documents | other collection |
|---|---:|---:|
| FEVER (19) | 91.6% | 0.0% |
| SciFact (11) | 58.2% | 20.0% |
| WikiContradict (8) | 100.0% | 0.0% |
| Verity (3) | 100.0% | 0.0% |

- **Wikipedia claims stay inside their article.** Each has a distinctive named entity.
- **SciFact claims spread across collections.** They share generic biomedical vocabulary ("inflammation", "cells", "risk") with the Acne, CD19 and Heart articles. Of their top-5 hits, 58% come from the abstracts that hold the evidence, 20% from those Wikipedia articles, and 22% from other abstracts.
- **This matters for the judge (M4).** A passage on the right topic from the wrong source is not evidence for the claim. Each document's provenance must be in front of the judge.

## 9. Can BM25 scores detect "insufficient evidence"?

| | top-1 score |
|---|---|
| answerable claims | mean 28.1, min 6.6 |
| insufficient claims | mean 20.4, max 41.8 |
| AUROC | **0.722** |

The highest-scoring insufficient claim is sci-14, at 41.8, above most answerable claims. Its claim is a long, specific statement about B-cell migration. The top-ranked abstract shares much of its vocabulary but describes migration to *follicular* niches and never mentions paracortical areas or oxysterols. **A retrieval score measures word overlap, not whether a passage addresses the claim.** INSUFFICIENT has to be decided by the judge, never by a score threshold. The first M2 run reached the same conclusion with AUROC 0.857; the score separation is even weaker here.

## 10. Successes and slow claims

No answerable claim is a complete failure: each has a relevant passage in the top 10. The four slowest (first relevant rank below 3):

| claim | first relevant rank | cause |
|---|---:|---|
| sci-11 "Hyperfibrinogenemia decreases rates of femoropopliteal bypass thrombosis." | 8 | **Vocabulary mismatch.** The claim says "hyperfibrinogenemia" and "thrombosis"; the evidence says "plasma fibrinogen concentration" and "graft occlusion" / "patency". The same abstract's title and objective, which repeat "femoropopliteal" and "bypass", take the top ranks |
| fever-03 "Elizabeth of York was the mother of Richard III." | 5 | **A related but non-evidential passage ranks first**: the rumour that Richard III meant to marry Elizabeth. The evidence ("Niece of King Richard III") is short |
| fever-14 "Yandex is a newspaper." | 5 | **Only the entity matches.** `newspaper` occurs nowhere, so Yandex passages compete on `yandex` alone, and term frequency and passage length decide the order |
| fever-23 "A near-Earth object is abbreviated as NGO." | 4 | **The distinctive term is false.** `ngo` occurs nowhere; the passages that rank are generic NEO text |

All four are *contradicted* claims. A false claim often uses a word the source never uses ("newspaper", "NGO", "mother", "decreases"), so the words that make it false carry no retrieval signal. This is the main reason contradicted claims score lowest: MRR 0.663, against 0.844 for supported claims.

## 11. Limitations

- **Small gold set.**
  - 41 answerable claims, completed by pooling from one system.
  - Most topics have 1–4 claims, which is too few to compare topics.
  - There are only **2 conflicts between sources**. The other 8 conflicts are inside one article, which is easier for lexical retrieval.
- **Datasets were adapted, not used as is.**
  - FEVER labels were re-derived against 2026 revisions, and four of them changed (see `data/eval/README.md`).
  - Some WikiContradict "contexts" were paraphrases or infobox text. The quotes used here are verbatim from the pinned revisions.
  - Results are therefore not comparable with published FEVER, SciFact or WikiContradict numbers.
- **SciFact NEI claims are easy negatives.** Their cited abstracts are on the topic but don't address the claim. A larger corpus would make them harder.
- **Relevance = quote containment.** A passage that says the same thing in other words is unjudged and counts as irrelevant.
- **Wikipedia re-rendering.** Old revisions render with current templates, so a re-fetch may not reproduce a hash. The committed texts are the reference.
- **Not tested:** query expansion, phrase or proximity matching, BM25F field weighting, and metadata filtering.

## 12. Recommendations

1. **Add dense and hybrid retrieval (M6) for recall on paraphrases.** Paraphrased claims score MRR 0.677, against 0.902 for lexical ones. Re-pool the gold set with the new system.
2. **Retrieve deeper and per source before judging.** All 10 conflicting claims had both sides in the top 20 but only 6 in the top 5. Cross-source evidence ranks lower than same-article evidence.
3. **Add more cross-source conflicts to the gold set.** Candidates: other Simple English / English pairs, and SciFact claims with abstracts on both sides. Also add a held-out split, so analyzer and parameter choices can be made without touching the test claims.
4. **Merge overlapping passages** in the result list (61 adjacent duplicates across 50 queries).
5. **Never use a BM25 score threshold to declare INSUFFICIENT** (AUROC 0.72).

## What changed from the first M2 run

The first run (commit `a8489c8`) used six documents on one topic, which was originally meant only as an illustration of a contested question. It reported MRR 0.611 before pooling and 0.736 after, on 37 answerable claims. That corpus has been replaced, so those numbers are **superseded and not comparable** with the ones above. The two reports differ in:

| | first run | this run |
|---|---|---|
| corpus | 6 documents, mostly PDF, one topic | 97 documents, HTML/JSON, 13 topics, committed and redistributable |
| claims | 44, written by the builders | 50, adapted from FEVER, SciFact and WikiContradict, plus 3 of our own |
| phrasing | annotated by hand | measured (lexical overlap ≥ 0.5) |
| analyzer default | PDF repair on | PDF repair off (it only makes false joins on this corpus) |

What carried over unchanged:
- the BM25 implementation and its exact parity with rank_bm25;
- quote-containment relevance and pooling;
- the finding that score thresholds cannot detect insufficient evidence;
- the finding that paraphrase is lexical retrieval's main weakness.
